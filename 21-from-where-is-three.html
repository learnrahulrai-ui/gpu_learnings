The story

Lecture 2 of MIT 6.S894 shows a graph of CPU cores from many years. Across the graph goes how big each core is, counted in transistors; up the graph goes how much work it finishes per tick of its clock, measured with a benchmark. A red straight line rises from the smallest core. The black points of the real cores bend away under it, and the last and largest point, labelled Raptor Cove, sits at about 12 times the size and 4 times the work per tick of the first. Beside the gap the slide writes "3x off trend".

The learner had been following the lecturer back through the processors of past decades, lost the thread at the Core 2 Duo, and could not say where the 3 came from. A slide later the lecturer concluded that for work with plenty of independent pieces, more and simpler cores use the same silicon better than fewer complex ones, and the learner could not see the step that led there.

What does the 3 compare, and what decides whether one big core or many small ones finishes a job first?

In plain words

Making a single processor bigger has stopped paying in proportion: twelve times the transistors gave four times the work per clock tick. The same area spent on twelve small processors gives twelve times the work per tick, but only on work that can be cut into twelve independent pieces. A job that cannot be cut goes faster on the one big processor.

For a manager: before buying more processors, find the share of the job that can be split; below a break-even share one fast processor wins, and above it many small ones do.
