The story

The Lab 2 program warp_scheduler.cu gives the GPU more and more threads and prints how many arithmetic operations per second it achieves. On the course's GPU, an NVIDIA RTX 4000 Ada, the number climbs as threads are added, as everyone expects. Then, at one step, it falls by about 40 percent.

At that step the program added only one eighth more work. The time did not grow by one eighth; it nearly doubled. A learner who believes "more threads, more speed" has no way to explain that, and a learner who knows the definition of every term involved usually has no way either, because the explanation needs several numbers put side by side.

The learner of this site had already separated two facts in a voice note: how many SMs a chip has does not tell you how many threads or schedulers are busy. What do the numbers say happened between the last good setting and the first bad one, and what would the same reasoning predict for the GTX 1650 in this laptop?

In plain words

A GPU reaches its full speed when each part of it has just enough independent work to never sit idle. Past that point, extra work cannot go any faster, and if the extra work does not fit on the chip at once it has to wait its turn, which can almost double the time for a small increase in work.

For a manager: the best batch size for a GPU job is a specific number that depends on the chip. A batch slightly too large can be far slower than one slightly too small, and the right number on one GPU is wrong on another.
