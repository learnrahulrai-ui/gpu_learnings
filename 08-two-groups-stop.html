08. Lab 2: one fat loop, two groups, when does it stop?

Pencil. Paper. Every blank ____ is yours. Open CHECK only after you wrote a number.

Start from zero

Page 04: one group of 8 lanes, one while loop; each lane's mask bit freezes its own count, and the loop breaks when all 8 bits are N. Page 07: putting a second group's ops in the same loop body fills idle cycles (20 cycles per pass for 9 ops of one group; 2 groups fit 18 ops into the same 20 cycles). Now the loop body is fat: two groups, two masks, two count registers, one loop. Real counts, row i = 40 of the 256 x 256 run:

group A = columns 208..215:  12   8   7   6   6   6   5   5
group B = columns 216..223:   5   5   5   5   5   5   5   5

Where you got stuck

"My while loop becomes longer and fatter. Then what is a pass? A pass is a collection of a fat while loop or a thin while loop?" / "It was already difficult to do for 8. Now I have to do for 16." / "My brain refuses to see that I actually solved the problem in lab 1."

Step 1: define a pass

A pass = one trip through the loop body. In the fat loop, one pass does lines A to F for group A and lines A to F for group B. One pass moves each of the 16 spots forward by one square-and-add.

while ( ... ) {                     <- one trip = one pass
    maskA = (x2A + y2A <= 4)        maskB = (x2B + y2B <= 4)
    itersA = itersA - maskA         itersB = itersB - maskB
    lines A..F on group A           lines A..F on group B
    ++iter;
}

Step 2: two wrong stopping rules

DO: Rule 1: stop when group B is all N. The loop runs ____ passes. Group A's counts come out as ____ . How many of A's 8 counts are wrong? ____ .
CHECK 
B is all N before pass 6 -> 5 passes
A gets min(5, true count): 5 5 5 5 5 5 5 5
wrong: lanes with true count > 5 -> 12, 8, 7, 6, 6, 6 -> 6 wrong
DO: Rule 2: run until group A is all N, but do the count update for B with no mask (itersB = itersB + 1 every pass). Passes = ____ . B's counts come out as ____ .
CHECK 
A is all N before pass 13 -> 12 passes
B without mask: 12 12 12 12 12 12 12 12   (all 8 wrong, true is 5)

Step 3: the rule that works is two separate rules

"When does the loop end" and "when does a lane's count stop" are two questions. Lab 1 already answered the second one: the mask subtraction freezes each lane by itself (page 04, step 2). The loop end is the only new part: continue while any of the 16 bits is Y, which is maskA OR maskB tested with testz.

DO: Passes = ____ . itersB after pass 5 = ____ , after pass 6 = ____ , after pass 12 = ____ . itersA after pass 12 = ____ .
CHECK 
loop ends before pass 13 (A's lane 0 is the last Y) -> 12 passes
itersB: after 5 -> 5 5 5 5 5 5 5 5 ; after 6 -> 5 ... (maskB is all N) ; after 12 -> 5 ...
itersA after 12 -> 12 8 7 6 6 6 5 5
all 16 counts true. Nothing new was invented for the counts: it is the lab 1 mask, twice.

Step 4: more waste, fewer cycles

Compare two layouts using page 07's model (20 cycles per pass for 1 group; 2 groups also 20 cycles per pass, because 18 starts fit in 20 cycles).

DO: Separate loops (A alone, then B alone): passes = 12 + ____ , lane-passes = ____ , cycles = ____ . Fat loop: passes = ____ , lane-passes = 16 x ____ = ____ , cycles = ____ . Useful lane-passes in both = ____ .
CHECK 
separate: 12 + 5 = 17 passes   lane-passes 8*12 + 8*5 = 136   cycles 17 * 20 = 340
fat     : 12 passes            lane-passes 16*12      = 192   cycles 12 * 20 = 240
useful  : (12+8+7+6+6+6+5+5) + (8*5) = 55 + 40 = 95
The fat loop wastes 56 more lane-passes and still finishes 100 cycles earlier.
Wasted lanes cost start slots; idle cycles cost time. Here time wins.

Step 5: when the fat loop gains almost nothing

Use the row-22 group from page 04 as group A (one lane at 1000, the cap) and the same B (all 5).

DO: Separate: cycles = (1000 + 5) x 20 = ____ . Fat: cycles = ____ x 20 = ____ . Saving in percent = ____ . Now pair A with another group that also runs 1000 passes: separate = ____ , fat = ____ , saving = ____ percent.
CHECK 
A=1000, B=5    : separate 20100, fat 20000, saving 100 / 20100 = 0.5 percent
A=1000, B=1000 : separate 40000, fat 20000, saving 50 percent
The fat loop pays off when the two groups need similar numbers of passes.
Which groups get paired is part of the design, not an afterthought.
