14. Lab 2: who writes which place, and who finishes last

Each line uses only what the lines above it made. The grey note says which line it takes from. Fill every ____ on paper, then open CHECK. Under each piece of code, a panel reads it in English line by line. Any unfamiliar word: words used on this site.

The story

Lab 2 Part 2 asks the program to use every core of the CPU and every SM of the GPU. The learner's first reaction was surprise: they thought Lab 2 was only about making the 8-at-once code faster, and suddenly there were threads to create and blocks to launch.

Splitting the picture between workers has a correctness side and a speed side. The correctness side is strict: every place of the picture must be written by exactly one worker, never two and never none. The speed side is easy to miss: the job ends when the slowest worker ends, so a split that is perfectly fair in places can be unfair in work.

The Lab 2 picture is 1024 by 1024 places, and the laptop has 6 CPU cores. Dividing 1024 by 6 does not even give a whole number. What split is correct, and of the correct ones, which finishes first?

In plain words

Sharing work among many workers has two rules. Everything must be done exactly once, and nobody should be left with the hardest part. In this picture the hard part sits in the middle bands, so handing out the lines of the picture in turns (worker 1, 2, ..., 6, then again) shares the effort far better than handing out solid bands of them.

For a manager: "we split it six ways" says nothing until you know whether the six shares are equal in effort, not in size.

The interviewer's next question

Both <<<7,256>>> and <<<14,128>>> make 56 warps. Can the 7-block launch keep all 14 SMs working? Does the 14-block launch guarantee one block per SM? Prove each answer without assuming how the hardware places blocks.

CHECK 
7 blocks: no. A block executes on one SM, so 7 blocks occupy at most 7 SMs at any moment.
14 blocks: no guarantee. The hardware chooses placement; if an SM has room for two of these blocks,
it may take two while another SM takes none. Only a measurement (or a profiler's per-SM activity)
shows the actual spread.
