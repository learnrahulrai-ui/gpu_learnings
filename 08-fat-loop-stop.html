08. Lab 2: when does the fat while loop stop?

Each line uses only what the lines above it made. The grey note says which line it takes from. Fill every ____ on paper, then open CHECK. Under each piece of code, a panel reads it in English line by line. Any unfamiliar word: words used on this site.

The story

The previous lab trick, putting two independent sets of 8 places into one loop, raises a practical problem the learner hit straight away. The two sets need different numbers of repetitions. A loop can only end once. When should it end?

The learner's complaint was honest: the loop is getting fatter, it was already hard with 8 places, now there are 16, and what even counts as one repetition? The answer turns out to be something the learner had already built in Lab 1 without recognising it: each set's own comparison result already stops its own counters. Only the end of the loop is new.

Once the stopping rule is settled, the cost: the fat loop clearly does more useless arithmetic. Does it finish sooner or later than two thin loops, and by how much on the whole picture?

Your words: "My while loop becomes longer and fatter. Then what is a pass? A pass is a collection of a fat while loop or a thin while loop?" / "It was already difficult to do for 8. Now I have to do for 16." / "My brain refuses to see that I actually solved the problem in lab 1."

In plain words

Combining two jobs into one loop means the loop ends when the slower job ends, so the faster job's share of the loop is wasted. It still pays when the hardware was otherwise idle, and it pays most when the two jobs are about the same size. Choosing which jobs to combine is part of the design.

For a manager: batching work together is cheapest when the batched items are alike. Batching one huge item with one tiny one buys almost nothing.
