The story

With the timer fixed, the Lab 2 prelab asks for one number: how many cycles does one multiply-add take on this GPU? The obvious method is to divide the measured cycles by the number of multiply-adds. The obvious method is wrong, and an interviewer who wants to separate people who measure from people who recite will check exactly this.

The learner's machine produced six measurements across three kinds of kernel: one chain of multiply-adds, two chains side by side, and four chains side by side, each at two lengths. Among them is one from a copy-paste bug that accidentally turned two chains into one long chain. That bug turned out to be useful data.

Each of these numbers is easy to compute with. The question is what they mean together: what does one multiply-add cost when it must wait for the previous one, what does it cost when it does not, and what part of every measurement is not about multiply-adds at all?

Read the code in English, line by line

In plain words

Every measurement contains the thing you want plus things you do not. Dividing by a count hides the unwanted part; subtracting two measurements removes it, but only when both measurements carry the same unwanted part. Here the learner's machine showed that "the same" means the same kernel, not merely the same timer.

The results themselves: on this GPU, a multiply-add that waits for the previous one costs 4 cycles, and with independent work available the card begins one every 2 cycles for 32 threads. Going from one chain to two doubled the speed; going from two to four did not change it.
