Lesson 10. Many threads per core, then everything together

This lesson covers parts 3 and 4 of Lab 2, where you write the multithread and full versions, then the _ilp versions that add independent sets on top, beginning from your part 1 and part 2 versions. Paragraphs with a red rule beside them are for your pencil; work each one before opening its CHECK.

Part 3 of Lab 2 puts more than one thread on each CPU core and more than one set of 32 threads on each of the GPU's schedulers, the circuits that pick which instruction begins next, and part 4 adds the independent sets of part 1 back in. The lab asks how much each step helps, which number of threads is best, and what decides it. Each technique gives a speedup when tried alone, and the natural expectation is that the speedups multiply. What do the three techniques have in common, and how do you know when adding more of one stops paying?

The question asked at this step was what makes someone who has memorized everything still fail here:

"What will make a student fail in each of the parts of the assignment due to memorization? Let's say there is somebody who has memorized all the things but still cannot reason it out."

Write a guess. On the laptop's GPU, with the Lab 2 loop, how many sets of 32 threads per scheduler are worth having before more stop helping: 1, about 2, or 8 or more?

With one set of 32 threads per scheduler, about 17.5 of every 35.5 cycles sit idle

The laptop's CPU has 6 cores with 2 hardware threads each, 12 in all. Its GPU has 14 SMs, the identical blocks of hardware the chip is built from, with 4 schedulers each, 56 in all, each holding up to 8 warps, NVIDIA's name for a set of 32 consecutive threads that begin each instruction together, so 448 warps in all. NVIDIA's Turing tuning guide gives a scheduler at most one instruction begun per cycle, and the prelab measured that a 32-thread float instruction takes 2 cycles to begin and that a float operation needing the previous result waits 4 cycles. The Lab 2 loop has 9 float instructions per repetition and a repeating chain of 4 operations, 16 cycles, and the laptop's baseline, one block of 32 threads and so one warp, took about 208 ms for 8,792,320 warp-repetitions, one repetition of the loop by the whole warp each.

Work out the time per warp-repetition, 208 ms / 8,792,320, and the cycles that is at an assumed 1.5 billion cycles per second. Set it beside the cycles to begin the 9 float instructions, 9 x 2, and the 16 cycles of the chain. How many cycles per repetition does the scheduler begin no float instruction?

CHECK 
208e-3 / 8,792,320 = 23.7 ns;  x 1.5 = 35.5 cycles per repetition
begins: 9 x 2 = 18 cycles;  chain: 16 cycles
idle: about 35.5 - 18 = 17.5 cycles per repetition

Neither the begins nor the chain explains 35.5 cycles; the rest goes to the compare, the branch, the count, and the waits around them.

A second warp on the same scheduler can begin its instructions in those idle cycles. Work out how many warps per scheduler it takes before the float begins themselves fill every cycle, 35.5 / 18, and the cycles of the scheduler per warp-repetition with 1, 2 and 4 warps.

CHECK 
35.5 / 18 = about 2 warps per scheduler
1 warp:  35.5 cycles
2 warps: the larger of 35.5 and 36, over 2 = 18 each
4 warps: the larger of 35.5 and 72, over 4 = 18 each

Past about 2 warps per scheduler the float begins are the limit, and more warps add nothing. This rests on the assumed clock, which a measurement has to replace.

A second hardware thread on a CPU core fills the same waiting as a second set

The 2 hardware threads of one CPU core share its arithmetic circuits, and either can begin instructions while the other waits, as AMD describes the simultaneous multithreading of its cores. For one 8-place set the core's chain takes 4 x 3 = 12 cycles per repetition and its 9 vector instructions take about 4.5 cycles to begin, from Agner Fog's published Zen 2 figures of a 3-cycle wait and at least 2 vector float instructions begun per cycle.

If one CPU thread already carries 2 or 3 independent sets that fill the 12-cycle chain, what can a second hardware thread on the same core still fill? And if each thread carries only one set?

CHECK

With the chain already filled, little: both threads compete for the same begins. With one set per thread, the second thread fills the waiting that a second set would have filled, so the two techniques fill the same idle cycles.

Chained speedup ratios always multiply to the total, so that check tests nothing

The Lab 2 tutor notes illustrate combining with times that were not measured: a baseline B of 120 ms; I, the baseline plus independent sets inside each thread, 60; C, many SMs, 24; T, many SMs and more threads per SM, 12; and F, T plus independent sets, 10.

Work out (B / C) x (C / T) x (T / F) and B / F. Then repeat with any other times, B = 97, C = 41, T = 13 and F = 7, and write the product as one fraction to see why. Finally work out the gain of the independent sets alone, B / I, and on top of everything else, T / F.

CHECK 
5 x 2 x 1.2 = 12 = B / F
97/41 x 41/13 x 13/7 = 97/7 = 13.86 = B / F
(B x C x T) / (C x T x F) = B / F, whatever the times
alone: 120 / 60 = 2;  on top of T: 12 / 10 = 1.2

The product cancels to B / F for any times, so as a check it cannot fail. The gain of the sets shrank from 2 alone to 1.2 on top, because T had already filled the same waiting.

Registers slow a kernel only when the work needs more warps than fit

An SM of the GTX 1650 has 65,536 registers, by NVIDIA's Turing tuning guide. Work out how many warps fit when each thread uses 72 registers, 65,536 / (72 x 32), and how many that is per scheduler; compare with the 2 per scheduler the work needs. Then the same at 128 registers per thread.

CHECK 
72 registers:  65,536 / 2,304 = 28.4 -> 28 warps, 7 per scheduler
128 registers: 65,536 / 4,096 = 16 warps, 4 per scheduler

Both leave more than the 2 per scheduler needed, so registers are not the limit here; they limit a kernel only when the work needs more warps per scheduler than fit, and -Xptxas -v reports a kernel's real use.

Writing parts 3 and 4, and the sweep that finds the number that is enough

Part 3 on the CPU, mandelbrot_cpu_vector_multicore_multithread, is the part 2 version with a thread count you can change, tried at 6, 12, 18 and 24 on the laptop. Part 3 on the GPU is mandelbrot_gpu_vector_multicore_multithread_single_sm, one block of up to 32 warps, then mandelbrot_gpu_vector_multicore_multithread_full, any number of blocks and warps. Part 4 adds 2 or 3 independent sets per thread on top, in the _ilp versions.

For the single-SM version, sweep warps per block through 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 and write the times. From the idle cycles above, where should the times stop falling?

CHECK

The calculation predicts the times falling from 1 warp to 4, when each scheduler has one; falling again, by up to 2 times, from 4 to 8, when each has two; and flat from 8 to 32. The laptop's sweep was not recorded, so yours is the first.

Write your best CPU thread count with its time, and your best GPU warps per scheduler with its time. Then write the gain of the independent sets alone, from your part 1, and their gain on top of your best part 3 version, part 4 against part 3.

CHECK

A gain on top near 1 means part 3 had already filled the waiting that the sets fill; a gain on top near the gain alone would mean they filled different idle cycles.

All three techniques fill the same idle cycles

Gathered together, one warp per scheduler leaves 17.5 of 35.5 cycles idle; about 2 warps per scheduler fill them, and more add nothing; a second hardware thread fills the same waiting as a second set; the chained product of speedups always matches the total, while the sets' gain shrank from 2 alone to 1.2 on top; and registers leave 7 or 4 warps per scheduler, more than needed.

Write one sentence answering the lab's last question: why do techniques that each help alone gain little together, and how many threads are enough?

CHECK

Independent sets inside a thread, more threads per core and more warps per scheduler all fill the same idle cycles; enough is the count at which the begins fill them, here about 2 warps per scheduler, and past it every technique adds work, not speed. Combining is tested by the gain on top, T / F, never by the chained product.

The reading "use the most threads the hardware allows" fails, because the useful number sits near 2 per scheduler against 8 held.

The Fall 2025 handout's fifth question, how much multithreading per core speeds up the CPU version, which count is best and why, is answered by the second hardware thread and your best count. The sixth, how the time in one block changes with warps beyond 4 up to 32, by the sweep and its predicted flattening at 8. The seventh, the same for warps per scheduler on the full GPU, by the 2 per scheduler and your best count. The eighth, what adding the independent sets back on top of many cores and many threads gains, and with how many sets, by the gain on top against the gain alone, with 2 sets and with 3.

A CPU version that slows down with more threads per core has threads competing for the same begins and paying more start-up; a GPU that stops improving at about 8 warps per block behaves as predicted; a set version that gains less on top of multithreading than alone fills the same idle cycles; and a check of chained speedups that succeeds proves nothing, since it always succeeds.

Without code, three different-sounding techniques, more work inside each thread, more threads per core and more sets of threads per scheduler, solve the same problem, hardware waiting for results. Once the waiting is filled none of them can help further, so combining them gives much less than multiplying their separate gains, and two optimizations worth 2 times each are worth far less than 4 until one is measured on top of the other.

The guess at the top has its answer: about 2, since one warp per scheduler leaves about 17.5 of 35.5 cycles per repetition idle at the assumed clock, and 2 warps fill them, after which the float begins are the limit.

A harder question to finish with. Your measurements show the single-SM time still falling from 8 to 16 warps per block. Which assumption above is the most likely to be wrong, and how would you check it?

CHECK

The clock, or the count of instructions. A clock above 1.5 GHz means more cycles per repetition and more idle cycles to fill, and instructions other than float ones, compares, integer adds and branches, also take begins, so the true limit sits below 18 cycles. Reading the clock during the measurement with nvidia-smi -q -d CLOCK checks the first, and counting every instruction of the loop in the cuobjdump listing, not only the float ones, checks the second.

The lab pages do speedups multiply, fixed by the chip, or made by the launch and a second place per thread work these numbers out at length, and the notes on combining speedups and the register budget hold the short versions.
