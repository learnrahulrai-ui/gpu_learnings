MIT 6.S894 Accelerated Computing: notes for self-learners

Topics: course. Labs 1 and 2, in course order. Each part of a lab below says what the lab asks and what was measured, then lists the short notes behind it. Each note is one idea: a few plain sentences, then numbered lines to calculate. No search box and no scripts: the notes are plain pages, so use your browser's find (Ctrl+F) on the list of all notes.

The course

MIT 6.S894, Accelerated Computing, is a graduate course from MIT EECS, taught in Fall 2025 by Jonathan Ragan-Kelley. Its pages are public at accelerated-computing.academy/fall25, and the starter code of every lab is public at github.com/accelerated-computing-class. The labs program NVIDIA GPUs in CUDA, beginning with the CPU for comparison.

These notes cover Lab 1 and Lab 2 as done by one learner outside MIT, on a laptop instead of the course machines: a Ryzen 5 4600H CPU and a GTX 1650 GPU. Every measured number comes from that laptop unless a line says otherwise. Where a note says "your output" or "your code", it means that learner's; redo each line with your own numbers if your machine differs.

Lab 1: the picture on one CPU core

The lab computes a 256 by 256 picture of the Mandelbrot set. For each place it repeats a short calculation at most 1000 times and stores how many repetitions it did. Measured on the laptop: 61.6 ms on one core, and the counts of all places add up to 16,274,722.

  • The middleman pair (cx, cy): from a place on the image paper to a point on the graph paper, and what a bigger picture changes
  • iters: repetitions until the while loop is left: what each number counts, and whether places are equal work

Full exercises: 01, 02, 03.

Lab 1: 8 places per CPU instruction

The same picture with AVX2 instructions, which act on 8 floats at once. Measured: 8.71 ms on one core, against 61.6 ms without them.

  • SIMD on the CPU: 8 places in one AVX2 instruction: what it costs when the 8 places need different numbers of repetitions
  • The mask, and why all ones reads as -1: how each of the 8 keeps its own count

Full exercises: 04, 05.

Lab 1: the picture on the GPU

The same picture on the GTX 1650, first with one GPU thread, then with one block of 32 threads. Measured: 403.435 ms and 17.732 ms.

  • SM: the block of hardware a GPU chip is built from: what the chip contains, and whether more circuits win by default
  • The 32-thread unit: what happens to threads that finish early
  • A program counter for every thread: why the 32-thread unit still matters
  • Nearby: 32 stores in 4 blocks of 32 bytes: the profiler's two counts, derived by hand

Full exercises: 00, 06, 11.

Lab 2 prelab: timing one multiply-add

The starter file fma_latency.cu reads the GPU's cycle counter around chains of multiply-adds. Measured on the laptop, in cycles: one chain 281 (64 multiply-adds) and 1055 (256), two chains 295 and 551, four chains 519 and 1031.

  • What sits between two clock reads: why the source code order does not say what is timed
  • Measuring by subtracting two lengths: the cost of one multiply-add, without the fixed part
  • Independent chains inside one thread: when extra chains stop helping

Full exercises: 09, 10.

Lab 2 prelab: how many threads keep the GPU busy

The starter file warp_scheduler.cu launches chains of multiply-adds in 48 x m blocks of 64 threads and reports operations per second. Reported for the course GPU in the learner's notes: 26.4575 x 10^12 at m = 8 and 15.9401 x 10^12 at m = 9.

  • Filling the 4-cycle wait: needed against held: how many threads a scheduler needs, against how many it can hold
  • A second round of blocks: the jump between m = 8 and m = 9
  • The register budget: when fewer threads fit, and when that matters

Full exercises: 12, 13, 17.

Lab 2: a bigger picture, split many ways

A 1024 by 1024 picture with at most 2000 repetitions per place, computed with several CPU threads and many GPU threads, then with independent sets of work inside each thread. Measured: 221.97 ms on the GPU for one block of 32 threads, after a sign error in the window was fixed.

  • Owner: exactly one writer per place: correct splits, fast splits
  • What the printed difference can hide: whether a broken version can look better than a correct one
  • Combining speedups: which comparison says whether a technique still helps

Full exercises: 07, 08, 14, 15, 16, 18.

Beyond the labs: the standard programs

Programs that every GPU course meets, worked out from the GTX 1650's published figures, with a line in each asking for your own measurement.

  • Operations per byte: whether memory or arithmetic sets the time when adding two arrays
  • Tiles in shared memory: how much reuse a matrix multiply needs, and whether a tile that size fits
  • Adding up an array: chain or tree: what the order of the adds changes

Full exercises: T1, T2, T3.
