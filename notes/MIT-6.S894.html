MIT 6.S894 Accelerated Computing, Labs 1 and 2, on your own computer

These are plain pages with no scripts, so there is no search box; your browser's find, Ctrl+F, works on the list of lessons and notes, and every unfamiliar word is explained in the words used on this site.

MIT 6.S894, Accelerated Computing, is a graduate course from MIT EECS, taught in Fall 2025 by Jonathan Ragan-Kelley. Its labs program NVIDIA GPUs in CUDA, beginning with the CPU for comparison; its pages are public at accelerated-computing.academy/fall25, and the starter code of every lab at github.com/accelerated-computing-class. This tutorial works through Lab 1 and Lab 2 outside MIT, on a laptop instead of the course machines, with a Ryzen 5 4600H CPU and a GTX 1650 GPU, one lesson at a time: set up, build each part of the lab, check that it is exactly right, measure it, and connect the numbers. Every number on these pages was measured on that laptop or computed from its results unless a sentence says otherwise, and every lesson makes you do the calculation yourself. The lessons are meant to be done in order, beginning with Lesson 0.

The Fall 2025 handouts for the two labs are at accelerated-computing.academy/fall25/labs/lab1 and accelerated-computing.academy/fall25/labs/lab2. The README files in the starter repositories still point to the Fall 2024 handouts, and only the Fall 2025 ones describe the prelab files fma_latency.cu and warp_scheduler.cu that the Lab 2 repository contains, so those are the ones to follow. The Lab 2 handout names the course machines, an AMD Ryzen 7 7700 CPU with 8 cores of 2 hardware threads each, and an NVIDIA RTX 4000 Ada GPU at 2.175 GHz built from 48 SMs, the identical blocks of hardware a GPU chip is made of, each with 4 schedulers, the circuits that pick which instruction begins next. Lab 1's vector part targets AVX-512, which the laptop's CPU does not have, and the lessons say at each step what changes with it.

Students enrolled in the course follow its collaboration policy. These pages describe the labs and work out their numbers, but the code and the answers handed in must be the student's own, and the lessons describe each piece to write without handing over finished lab code.

You need C++, a pencil, and for the GPU half an NVIDIA GPU

These pages are for self-learners following MIT 6.S894 from its public pages, without the course machines, staff or grading, and especially for people who can recite the definitions and still get stuck connecting them. Reading C++ and calculating with pencil and paper is all that is assumed. No earlier GPU experience is, because every word is defined before it is used and every piece of code is followed by a reading of it in plain English.

The computer needs Linux, or Windows with WSL2, with g++ for C++17 and git. The CPU vector parts need a CPU with AVX2, 8 floats per instruction, or AVX-512, 16. The GPU parts need an NVIDIA GPU with its driver and the CUDA toolkit, which brings nvcc and cuobjdump, and the profiler steps need Nsight Compute, ncu. The laptop behind every measured number has a Ryzen 5 4600H, with 6 cores, AVX2 and no AVX-512, and a GTX 1650 of the generation NVIDIA calls Turing, with 14 SMs, on Ubuntu with CUDA 12.4.

Every lesson asks for your guess first and sets it against the laptop last

A lesson opens with the problem in plain sentences, ending on the question it answers, then quotes the chats in which the labs were first worked through at the point where they got hard, and asks for a guess, a number or a yes or no, written down before any calculation. Each section after that is headed by the result it establishes. Paragraphs with a red rule beside them are for your pencil, and their answers stay hidden under CHECK until you open them. Every number a paragraph uses was given or computed earlier on the same page, and the paragraph says where it came from, whether measured on the laptop, computed from the lab's code, taken from a published figure, or assumed.

Where a lab part asks for code, the lesson describes each piece to write and the exact commands that compile and execute it, each followed by a reading in plain English, and then asks for your printed difference and your time beside the laptop's; where the laptop measured nothing, the lesson says so and leaves the measurement to you. Near its end a lesson gathers its results and asks for the one sentence that connects them, names the reading of them that fails and the number that refutes it, describes the failures that look like success together with their causes, answers the handout's questions from what came before, says in plain words what the results mean for someone who does not program, sets your guess against the measured value, and finishes with a harder follow-up question.

Lessons 0 to 10 go from an empty function to every technique combined

Before Lab 1, Lesson 0, Set up your machine and read the starter program, builds nothing yet and works out why an empty function prints 0.248. Lab 1, part 1, takes two lessons. In Lesson 1, The reference program, by hand, you add a total of the repetition counts of all places and find 97.7 percent of the work in 24.3 percent of the places, and in Lesson 2, The picture on one GPU thread, you write mandelbrot_gpu_scalar and work out why it took 403 ms against 61.6 ms on one CPU core.

Lab 1, part 2, also takes two. Lesson 3, Vector code on the CPU: 8 places per instruction, has you write mandelbrot_cpu_vector and explains 7.07 times, not 8, and Lesson 4, The picture on 32 GPU threads, has you write mandelbrot_gpu_vector and explains 22.75 times, not 32, together with the profiler's 2,048 and 8,192.

The Lab 2 prelab takes the next two. In Lesson 5, Timing one multiply-add, you fill the three kernels of fma_latency.cu and find 4 cycles, then 2, and in Lesson 6, How many threads keep the GPU busy, you execute and read the warp_scheduler.cu sweep, where 112 sets of 32 threads are needed and 448 held.

Lab 2 itself takes the last four. Lesson 7, The Lab 2 picture: port, check, time, moves your Lab 1 code into the Lab 2 files and shows why only an exact 0 counts. Lesson 8, Independent work inside one thread, covers part 1, the _ilp versions, and the times 461, 231 and 208 ms. Lesson 9, Many CPU cores, many SMs, covers part 2, the _multicore versions, with 4.72 in bands against 5.98 in turns. Lesson 10, Many threads per core, then everything together, covers parts 3 and 4, the _multithread and combined versions, where about 2 sets of 32 threads per scheduler are enough and speedups do not multiply.

Short notes hold one idea each, in the order the labs meet them

Each note is a short reference page for one idea, with its own worked lines, and it points to the lessons and the full exercise pages that work the idea out at length.

Lab 1 begins with a 256 by 256 picture of the Mandelbrot set on one CPU core: for each place the program repeats a short calculation at most 1000 times and stores how many repetitions it did, and on the laptop that took 61.6 ms, with the counts of all places adding up to 16,274,722. Its notes, the middleman pair (cx, cy) and iters, the repetitions until the while loop is left, ask in turn how a place (i, j) of the picture becomes the point (cx, cy) that the loop works on and what a bigger picture changes, and what each count counts and whether places are equal work. Lessons 0 and 1 and the exercise pages 01, 02 and 03 work them out in full.

Its vector part computes the same picture with AVX2 instructions, which act on 8 floats at once, in 8.71 ms on one core against 61.6 ms without them. Its notes, SIMD on the CPU, 8 places in one AVX2 instruction, and the mask, and why all ones reads as -1, ask in turn what an instruction on 8 places costs when they need different numbers of repetitions, and how each of the 8 keeps its own count. Lesson 3 and the exercise pages 04 and 05 work them out in full.

The same picture on the GTX 1650 took 403.435 ms with one GPU thread and 17.732 ms with one block of 32 threads. Its notes, the SM, the block of hardware a GPU chip is built from, the 32-thread unit, a program counter for every thread and nearby, 32 stores in 4 blocks of 32 bytes, ask in turn what the chip contains and whether more circuits win by default, what happens to threads that finish early, why the 32-thread unit still matters when every thread has a program counter, and how the profiler's two counts follow by hand. Lessons 2 and 4 and the exercise pages 00, 06 and 11 work them out in full.

The Lab 2 prelab file fma_latency.cu reads the GPU's cycle counter around chains of multiply-adds, and the laptop measured, in cycles, 281 for one chain of 64 multiply-adds and 1055 for one of 256, 295 and 551 for two chains, and 519 and 1031 for four. Its notes, what sits between two clock reads, measuring by subtracting two lengths and independent chains inside one thread, ask in turn why the order of the source code does not say what is timed, what one multiply-add costs without the fixed part, and when extra chains stop helping. Lesson 5 and the exercise pages 09 and 10 work them out in full.

The second prelab file, warp_scheduler.cu, launches chains of multiply-adds in 48 x m blocks of 64 threads and reports operations per second, and the notes kept from Lab 2 report 26.4575 x 10^12 for the course GPU at m = 8 and 15.9401 x 10^12 at m = 9. Its notes, filling the 4-cycle wait, needed against held, a second round of blocks and the register budget, ask in turn how many threads a scheduler needs against how many it can hold, what makes the jump between m = 8 and m = 9, and when fewer threads fit and when that matters. Lesson 6 and the exercise pages 12, 13 and 17 work them out in full.

Lab 2 proper computes a 1024 by 1024 picture with at most 2000 repetitions per place, with several CPU threads and many GPU threads, then with independent sets of work inside each thread; on the laptop's GPU one block of 32 threads took 221.97 ms, once a sign error in the window was fixed. Its notes, a single writer per place, what the printed difference can hide and combining speedups, ask in turn what makes a split correct and what makes it fast, whether a broken version can look better than a correct one, and which comparison says whether a technique still helps. Lessons 7, 8, 9 and 10 and the exercise pages 07, 08, 14, 15, 16 and 18 work them out in full.

Beyond the labs, the standard programs that every GPU course meets are worked out from the GTX 1650's published figures, each with a line asking for your own measurement. Their notes, operations per byte, tiles in shared memory and adding up an array, chain or tree, ask in turn whether memory or arithmetic sets the time when adding two arrays, how much reuse a matrix multiply needs and whether a tile that size fits, and what the order of the adds changes. The exercise pages T1, T2 and T3 work them out in full.
