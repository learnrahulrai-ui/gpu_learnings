GPU Learnings / notes

Lessons and notes

The lessons are the tutorial: done in order, they take you through Labs 1 and 2 of MIT 6.S894 on your own machine. The notes are short reference pages, one idea each, for looking things up. These are plain pages with no scripts, so there is no search box: press Ctrl+F (Cmd+F on a Mac) on this page to find a word.

Lessons, in order

  • Lesson 0. Set up your machine and read the starter program: Get the Lab 1 and Lab 2 starter code, compile it on your own computer, and read what the untouched program prints, including the 0.248 that an empty function prints.
  • Lesson 1. The reference program, by hand: Read mandelbrot_cpu_scalar and main from the Lab 1 starter, work one place through the loop by hand, follow how the new y gets 2 * a * b out of w, and add up and split the repetitions of all 65,536 places.
  • Lesson 2. The picture on one GPU thread: Write the first kernel and its launch, check that it prints exactly 0, and work out why one GPU thread took 403 ms against 61.6 ms on one CPU core.
  • Lesson 3. Vector code on the CPU: 8 places per instruction: Write the vector version with AVX2 (8 floats) or AVX-512 (16): the cx of 8 places from one number, a loop that 8 places leave at different times, and why the result was 7.07 times, not 8.
  • Lesson 4. The picture on 32 GPU threads: Split the places between 32 threads with threadIdx.x, prove with the profiler's own counts that each store instruction lands in 4 blocks of 32 bytes, and explain 22.75 times instead of 32.
  • Lesson 5. Timing one multiply-add: Time chains of multiply-adds with the GPU's cycle counter: what sits between the clock reads, why 281 / 64 is not the answer, and how 4 cycles become 2 with a second chain.
  • Lesson 6. How many threads keep the GPU busy: Read warp_scheduler.cu's sweep: why the course GPU peaks at 768 sets of 32 threads, why the laptop needs only 112, what the literal 48 does on 14 SMs, and what 12.5 percent more work costing 87 percent more time means.
  • Lesson 7. The Lab 2 picture: port, check, time: Move your Lab 1 code into the Lab 2 files: the new window constants, the changed line, and why only an exact 0 counts: 0.045 for a sign error, 0.00756 for a harmless rounding change, 0.0000448 for a missing line.
  • Lesson 8. Independent work inside one thread: Put a second independent set of places in each loop: which line waits, when the fat while loop may stop, and why the CPU should gain while the GPU at one block of 32 threads did not: 461 ms, 231 ms, 208 ms.
  • Lesson 9. Many CPU cores, many SMs: Split the picture between CPU threads and GPU blocks so that every place has one owner and every owner similar work: 4.72 in bands against 5.98 in turns, blockIdx.x, and launches that use only 7 of 14 SMs.
  • Lesson 10. Many threads per core, then everything together: More threads per core and more sets of 32 threads per scheduler fill the same waiting as independent work inside a thread: how to find the number that is enough, why speedups do not multiply, and when registers matter.

All notes, A to Z

  • A program counter for every thread: Since Volta, each thread keeps its own program counter; the 32-thread unit still matters, because equal program counters let one instruction serve 32 threads.
  • A second round of blocks: On the course GPU, 12.5 percent more work took 87 percent more time. Blocks that do not fit wait for a second round.
  • Adding up an array: chain or tree: A plain loop over 20 million floats is a chain of 20 million adds and stops counting at 16,777,216; a tree has 25 levels and gets 20,000,000.
  • Combining speedups: The product of step-by-step speedups always equals the total, so it tests nothing. Compare a technique's gain alone with its gain on top of the others.
  • Filling the 4-cycle wait: needed against held: A scheduler of the GTX 1650 holds up to 8 sets of 32 threads, but chains of multiply-adds need only 2. Why filling the chip to its maximum is not the target.
  • Independent chains inside one thread: A multiply-add that needs the previous result waits 4 cycles; a second chain fills the wait; a fourth chain finds nothing left to fill.
  • iters: repetitions until the while loop is left: What each number of the Mandelbrot picture counts, why it ranges from 1 to 1000, and why equal numbers of places are not equal work.
  • Measuring by subtracting two lengths: 281 / 64 = 4.39 is not the cost of one multiply-add; (1055 - 281) / (256 - 64) = 4.03 is. And a single division makes four chains look faster than two.
  • Nearby: 32 stores in 4 blocks of 32 bytes: The profiler's 2,048 requests and 8,192 sectors derived by hand, and a split of places that would move 8 times more.
  • Operations per byte: Adding two arrays does 0.083 adds per byte moved; the GTX 1650 needs about 10.5 to keep its arithmetic busy, so memory sets the time.
  • Owner: exactly one writer per place: A correct split gives every place one owner; a fast split also gives every owner similar work: 4.72 against 5.98 on the Lab 2 picture.
  • SIMD on the CPU: 8 places in one AVX2 instruction: Why 8 places per instruction gave 7.07 times, not 8, and why the picture, not the instructions, decides it.
  • SM: the block of hardware a GPU chip is built from: What one SM of the GTX 1650 contains, 896 FP32 units against the CPU's 48 floats per instruction, and why the CPU still finished first.
  • The 32-thread unit: NVIDIA calls it a warp: one instruction begins for 32 threads at once, and threads that are done wait. Why 32 threads gave 22.75 times, not 32.
  • The mask, and why all ones reads as -1: How each of 8 indexes keeps its own count inside one AVX2 while loop, with no if.
  • The middleman pair (cx, cy): How a place (i, j) on the image paper becomes a point (cx, cy) on the graph paper, and what img_size does and does not change.
  • The register budget: Every SM has 65,536 registers, shared by all the threads it holds: 64 each at 1,024 threads, and when using more actually slows a kernel.
  • Tiles in shared memory: The plain matrix multiply reads 8.6 billion bytes for 2.1 billion operations; tiles of T by T divide that by T; T = 84 would need 55 KB of an SM's 64 KB.
  • What sits between two clock reads: The source code put an add before the timer; the machine listing put it after. How to know what a timing contains, and how giving the clock read inputs fixes it.
  • What the printed difference can hide: A single wrong place prints 0.000000000477; a missing line of the picture prints 169 times less than a harmless rounding change.
