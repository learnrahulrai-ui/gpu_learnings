Lesson 7. The Lab 2 picture: port, check, time

This lesson comes before part 1 of Lab 2 and moves your Lab 1 vector versions into the Lab 2 files, so it needs your Lab 1 vector code for the CPU and the GPU. Paragraphs with a red rule beside them are for your pencil; work each one before opening its CHECK.

Lab 2 computes a bigger and deeper picture: 1024 by 1024 places, up to 2000 repetitions each, inside a window one ten-thousandth across. Its files invite you to paste in your Lab 1 vector code for comparison, updated to the new window constants, and the move brings two small changes. The window is set by window_zoom, window_x and window_y instead of 2.5, 2.0 and 1.25, and one line of the loop is written w - (x2 + y2) + cy instead of w - x2 - y2 + cy, which is the same in exact arithmetic. When a port prints a small difference instead of 0, is it a bug or rounding, and how can anyone tell?

The worry at this step was how anyone proves such a port correct rather than guessing:

"Let's say somebody in an interview, instead of asking me to memorize, gives me this program, and they don't like guesswork. They want me to prove using debugger tools ... why is it slow, where it is going slowness."

Write a guess. Lab 2 prints a single wrong place as 1 / (1024 x 1024 x 2000). A first version forgets a whole value of i, 1024 places; a second only changes rounding. Which prints the smaller number?

Lab 2's window is 0.0001 across, 25,000 times less than Lab 1's 2.5

constexpr float window_zoom = 1.0 / 10000.0f;
constexpr float window_x = -0.743643887 - 0.5 * window_zoom;
constexpr float window_y = 0.131825904 - 0.5 * window_zoom;
constexpr uint32_t default_max_iters = 2000;
float cx = (float(j) / float(img_size)) * window_zoom + window_x;
float cy = (float(i) / float(img_size)) * window_zoom + window_y;

These lines from the Lab 2 starter, the same in its CPU and GPU files, say that the window, the part of the plane of points (cx, cy) that the picture shows, is 1 / 10000 = 0.0001 across, that its left edge is -0.743643887 minus half of that and its top edge 0.131825904 minus half of that, and that each place gets at most 2000 repetitions; cx is j divided by img_size, times 0.0001, plus the left edge, and cy the same with i and the top edge.

Work out window_x and window_y. With img_size = 1024, work out cx at j = 0 and at j = 1023, and how many times smaller 0.0001 is than Lab 1's 2.5.

CHECK 
window_x = -0.743643887 - 0.00005 = -0.743693887
window_y =  0.131825904 - 0.00005 =  0.131775904
j = 0:    cx = -0.743693887
j = 1023: 1023 / 1024 x 0.0001 - 0.743693887 = -0.743593985
2.5 / 0.0001 = 25,000

In the IEEE 754 32-bit format, floats between 0.5 and 1 are 2 to the power -24 = 0.0000000596 apart. Work out the step between neighbouring values of cx, 0.0001 / 1024, and that step in float steps.

CHECK 
0.0001 / 1024 = 0.0000000977 per place
0.0000000977 / 0.0000000596 = 1.64 float steps

Neighbouring places are only 1 or 2 float steps apart, so a rounding of one step anywhere in a repetition moves a point by more than half the distance to its neighbour.

Rewriting line B changes the counts at 23.8 percent of the places

float y = w - (x2 + y2) + cy;

Read in English, the Lab 2 form of line B subtracts the sum of x2 and y2 from w, then adds cy; the Lab 1 form, float y = w - x2 - y2 + cy;, subtracted x2 and y2 one at a time.

Computed in 32-bit fractions from the starter's constants, the two forms give different counts at 249,479 of the 1,048,576 places, a total difference of 15,852,362 repetitions, although they are equal in exact arithmetic. Every place of the Lab 2 picture needs between 69 and 2000 repetitions, and all of them add up to 138,219,822.

Work out the share of places whose count changed.

CHECK

249,479 / 1,048,576 = 23.8 percent, which fits the 1.64 float steps above: in a picture this zoomed, a one-step rounding difference grows over hundreds of repetitions into a different count.

A missing line of the picture prints 169 times less than a rounding change

int64_t diff = 0;
for (uint32_t i = 0; i < img_size; i++) {
    for (uint32_t j = 0; j < img_size; j++) {
        diff +=
            abs(int(result[i * img_size + j]) - int(ref_result[i * img_size + j]));
    }
}
return diff / double(img_size * img_size * max_iters);

Read in English, the check sets a 64-bit sum to 0, visits every place, adds the size of the gap between the version's count and the reference's count, and returns that sum divided by img_size times img_size times max_iters.

Work out the divisor, 1024 x 1024 x 2000, and what a single place wrong by 1 prints. Then work out what the line B change prints, 15,852,362 over the divisor, and what a version prints that forgets the cheapest value of i and leaves its 1024 places at 0, a total difference of 93,904. Which looks better, and by how much?

CHECK 
divisor: 2,097,152,000
one place wrong by 1:  1 / 2,097,152,000 = 0.000000000477
line B change:  15,852,362 / 2,097,152,000 = 0.00756
missing line:       93,904 / 2,097,152,000 = 0.0000448

The broken version prints about 169 times less than the harmless rounding change.

The first port of this picture on the laptop had the sign of window_y wrong and printed 0.045. Work out the total gap, 0.045 x 2,097,152,000, and the gap per place. The fixed version printed exactly 0, at 221.97 ms on the GPU with one block of 32 threads.

CHECK

0.045 x 2,097,152,000 = 94,371,840 repetitions, 90 per place. A wrong window moves the whole picture, so its number is large and obvious; the dangerous bugs move a few places and print tiny numbers, as the missing line shows.

Porting the code, and the only result that counts as correct

In both Lab 2 files, uncomment the optional vector block, which keeps #define HAS_VECTOR_IMPL, paste your Lab 1 vector functions into it, replace 2.5, 2.0 and 1.25 by window_zoom, window_x and window_y, write line B in the Lab 2 form, and in the CPU version make the step between neighbouring cx window_zoom / img_size.

mkdir -p out
g++ -O3 -march=native -std=c++17 -pthread mandelbrot_cpu_2.cpp -o mandelbrot_cpu_2
nvcc -O3 -arch=sm_75 -std=c++17 mandelbrot_gpu_2.cu -o mandelbrot_gpu_2
./mandelbrot_cpu_2 && ./mandelbrot_gpu_2

In the lab2 folder, the first line makes the folder for the pictures, as in Lab 1. The second compiles the CPU file, with -pthread adding the POSIX threads library that its later parts use. The third compiles the GPU file for compute capability 7.5, and the fourth executes both, each timing every version it has and printing its difference.

Write the differences printed for your CPU vector version and your GPU vector version. If either is not exactly 0, which of the cases above does its size suggest?

CHECK

Both must be exactly 0. Around 0.045 points to the window, a sign or an edge; around 0.0076 to line B in the Lab 1 form; below 0.0001 to a few places, or a whole line of the picture, missing.

Since the average alone cannot tell these apart, add your own count of differing places and print the first (i, j) that differs, with both counts.

Separate executions of one version already differ by 7.4 percent

The Lab 2 starter times each CPU version 10 times, 1 call each, and prints the smallest. The GPU file sets the counts separately for each version: the vector baseline is timed 1 time with 4 launches, the ILP version 1 time with 2, and every later version 3 times with 5.

The laptop's GPU vector baseline printed 221.97, 222.90, 207.55, 208.67, 221.11, 207.87 and 207.82 ms in seven separate executions. Work out the spread, (222.90 - 207.55) / 207.55, and from how many numbers the baseline's smallest is chosen. Then write your own executions.

CHECK

The spread is 7.4 percent. The baseline is timed once, so its smallest is chosen from one number and is that number. Versions can only be compared across several executions each, with the numbers compared stated.

In a zoomed picture, correct means exactly 0 against the reference's own lines

Gathered together, the window is 0.0001 across, neighbouring places are 1.64 float steps apart, a rewrite that is exact in arithmetic changes 23.8 percent of the counts, a missing line prints 169 times less than that rewrite, a wrong window prints 90 repetitions per place, and separate executions differ by 7.4 percent.

Write one sentence on what correct and faster have to mean before any Lab 2 version is compared with another.

CHECK

Neighbouring places are a float step or two apart, so any change in rounding changes counts across a quarter of the picture; correct has to mean exactly 0 against the reference's own line forms, confirmed by a count of differing places, and faster has to mean a gap larger than the 7 percent that separate executions already show.

The reading "a tiny average difference is close enough" fails, because a missing line of the picture prints 169 times less than a harmless rounding change.

A difference near 0.045 means wrong window constants, often a sign; one near 0.0076 means line B still in the Lab 1 form; one below 0.0001 but not 0 means missing places, such as a whole value of i. A speedup that changes from one execution to the next is the 7 percent by which executions differ on their own, so compare several of each.

Without code, Lab 2 zooms 25,000 times deeper into the same picture, and at that depth neighbouring points differ by only one or two steps of the computer's number format, so a harmless-looking rewrite of one line changes the result at a quarter of the places. Only an exact match counts as correct, and a summary error figure can rank a seriously broken version above a harmless one, so the question to ask is how many results differ, not only by how much on average.

The guess at the top has its answer: the version missing a line of the picture prints 0.0000448, 169 times less than the rounding change's 0.00756.

A harder question to finish with. A port prints 0.000000000477. Is it correct, and what comes next?

CHECK

It is not: 0.000000000477 is exactly one place wrong by 1. The next step is a count of mismatched places, with the first one's i, j and both counts printed, and then the cause, either a real bug or a rounding difference such as a reordered line B, which has to match the reference before any speed comparison.

The lab pages what the two printed numbers can hide and which line waits for which line work these numbers out at length, and the notes on what the printed difference can hide and on a single writer per place hold the short versions.
