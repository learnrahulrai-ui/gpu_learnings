Lesson 6. How many threads keep the GPU busy

This lesson covers part 0 of Lab 2, the prelab file warp_scheduler.cu. There is nothing new to write, only its sweep to execute and read, on an NVIDIA GPU. Paragraphs with a red rule beside them are for your pencil; work each one before opening its CHECK.

The second prelab file launches more and more threads, each doing one long chain of multiply-adds, and prints operations per second for each launch; the lab asks when the GPU reaches its largest rate and what the multiply-add's wait says about it. The file was written for the course GPU, with that GPU's count of SMs, the identical blocks of hardware the chip is built from, typed in as the number 48. On a laptop with 14 SMs it still compiles and executes, and its table looks as clean as the course GPU's. How many threads does a GPU need to stay busy, and how does that differ from how many it can hold?

The first thing to settle at this step was which of these numbers belong to the chip at all:

"Confirm me this part, okay? I have 14 SMs. Yes or no?" / "just because I have SMs doesn't mean I know how many threads or schedulers you run. That is a different piece of information, yes or no?" / "Okay, so now what exactly I have to do?"

Write a guess. The GTX 1650 has 14 SMs with 4 schedulers each, the circuits that pick which instruction begins next. How many sets of 32 threads does it need to keep every one of its arithmetic circuits busy with chains of multiply-adds: 56, 112 or 448?

Course GPU and laptop have 192 and 56 schedulers

The Fall 2025 Lab 2 handout names the course GPU, an NVIDIA RTX 4000 Ada at 2.175 GHz with 48 SMs. Each SM handles the threads of a block in consecutive sets of 32, beginning each instruction for all 32 at once, and NVIDIA calls such a set a warp; each SM has 4 schedulers, which pick the warp whose instruction begins, 192 in all, and each scheduler holds up to 12 warps. NVIDIA's Ada specification gives each SM of that GPU 128 float circuits. The laptop's GTX 1650, of the generation NVIDIA calls Turing, has 14 SMs with 4 schedulers each, 56 in all, and NVIDIA's Turing tuning guide gives it 64 float circuits per SM and at most 32 warps and 16 blocks per SM.

uint32_t block_size = default_tile_size * num_threads_per_block;
dim3 grid(N / block_size);
tail_latency_fma<default_num_ops, default_tile_size><<<grid, block>>>(in, out, N);
int N = block_size * 48 * multiplier;
double flops = ((double)N * default_num_ops * 2) / time_in_seconds;
uint32_t warps = (N / block_size) * (num_threads_per_block / 32);

Read in English, a block handles default_tile_size, 32 numbers per thread, times num_threads_per_block, 64 threads by default, so 2,048 numbers; the grid has N / 2,048 blocks, in whole-number division; the kernel is launched on that grid with the default tile size and number of updates fixed in, so the tile size handed to this launch function is never used; N, the numbers for one setting of the sweep, is the block size times 48 times the multiplier, with the 48 typed in; the printed rate is N times default_num_ops times 2, each multiply-add counted as 2 operations, over the time in seconds; and the printed warps are the blocks times the threads per block divided by 32.

From the starter file itself, default_num_ops is 263 x 20 = 5,260, so each number goes through tmp += tmp * 3.0f 5,260 times, each time needing the value before. N is 2,048 x 48 x m numbers, for multipliers m from 1 to 48, and each line of output gives the warps and the rate.

For a multiplier m, write the number of blocks and the number of warps. Then work them out for m = 8, together with the warps per scheduler on the course GPU.

CHECK 
blocks = 2,048 x 48 x m / 2,048 = 48m;   warps = 2 x 48m = 96m
m = 8: 384 blocks, 768 warps, 768 / 192 = 4 per scheduler

A 4-cycle wait needs 4 warps per scheduler on the course GPU and 2 on the laptop

Every warp here is a single chain of multiply-adds, and a multiply-add that needs the previous one waits 4 cycles, measured on Turing and assumed the same on Ada. On the course GPU each scheduler has 128 / 4 float circuits; work out how many cycles 32 threads need to begin a multiply-add, how many warps per scheduler keep the circuits busy, and how many for the whole GPU. Then the same for the laptop, whose begin time the prelab measured as 2 cycles.

CHECK 
course GPU: 128 / 4 = 32 circuits per scheduler -> 32 threads begin in 1 cycle
            4 / 1 = 4 warps per scheduler -> 192 x 4 = 768 warps
laptop:     64 / 4 = 16 circuits per scheduler -> 2 cycles (measured: 2)
            4 / 2 = 2 warps per scheduler -> 56 x 2 = 112 warps

On the laptop, warp A begins at cycle 0, warp B at cycle 2, and warp A's next multiply-add is ready at cycle 4, so a third warp finds no free cycle.

Work out the most each GPU holds, 192 x 12 warps and 14 x 32 warps, and the share of that the chains need.

CHECK

The course GPU holds 2,304 warps and needs 768, 33 percent; the laptop holds 448 and needs 112, 25 percent. The most a chip holds is a ceiling, and the number the work needs comes from the wait and the begin time.

On the course GPU, 12.5 percent more work cost 87 percent more time

Rates reported for the course GPU in the notes kept from Lab 2: 26.4575 x 10^12 operations per second at m = 8 and 15.9401 x 10^12 at m = 9. Work out the operations per block, 2 x 2,048 x 5,260, the time at each multiplier as operations over rate, and the GPU's full rate, 6,144 circuits x 2 x 2.175 billion per second, with the share of it reached at m = 8.

CHECK 
per block: 2 x 2,048 x 5,260 = 21,544,960 operations
m = 8: 384 x 21,544,960 = 8,273,264,640 / 26.4575e12 = 0.3127 ms
m = 9: 432 x 21,544,960 = 9,307,422,720 / 15.9401e12 = 0.5839 ms
full rate: 6,144 x 2 x 2.175e9 = 26.73e12;  26.4575 / 26.73 = 99 percent

Work out the ratio of work and the ratio of time from m = 8 to m = 9. Then test a guess that no measurement has confirmed: an SM holds at most 8 blocks of this kernel at once, so 48 x 8 = 384 blocks fit and the extra 48 at m = 9 wait for a second round. If that round costs as much as the first, the time is 2 x 0.3127 ms; set it beside 0.5839.

CHECK

Work grows 432 / 384 = 1.125 times and time 0.5839 / 0.3127 = 1.867 times. The second round predicts 0.6254 ms, 6.6 percent from 0.5839, which supports the guess without proving it; asking CUDA for the blocks one SM holds, with cudaOccupancyMaxActiveBlocksPerMultiprocessor for this kernel at 64 threads per block, settles it.

On 14 SMs the literal 48 spreads the blocks unevenly

On the laptop the file still multiplies by 48. For m = 1 it launches 48 blocks on 14 SMs; write 48 as 3 x 14 plus a remainder, and say how many SMs get 4 blocks and how many get 3. The output then says 96 warps; work out what that is per scheduler on the laptop and on the course GPU.

CHECK 
48 = 3 x 14 + 6: 6 SMs hold 4 blocks (8 warps), 8 SMs hold 3 (6 warps)
96 / 56  = 1.71 warps per scheduler on the laptop
96 / 192 = 0.5 warps per scheduler on the course GPU

The same printed line describes a different point of each GPU's curve, so the two tables cannot be compared line by line.

On your own GPU the sweep takes two commands in the lab2 folder.

nvcc -O3 -arch=sm_75 -std=c++17 warp_scheduler.cu -o warp_scheduler
./warp_scheduler > sweep.csv

The first line compiles the sweep for compute capability 7.5, and the second executes it and saves its table in sweep.csv, one line per multiplier, with the warps and then the operations per second.

The laptop's circuits are all busy from about 112 warps, which the file's steps of 96 warps reach between m = 1 and m = 2. In your sweep, find the first line within 5 percent of the largest rate and write its warps and that rate, together with your GPU's full rate, 896 x 2 x your clock.

CHECK

The rate should stop growing near the m = 2 line, 192 warps, since 96 is below the 112 needed and 192 is above it. The laptop's own sweep was not recorded, so yours is the first measurement of it; at an assumed 1.5 billion cycles per second the full rate is 896 x 2 x 1.5e9 = 2.69 x 10^12.

Replace the 48 by the SM count the GPU reports, the field multiProcessorCount that cudaGetDeviceProperties fills, as the CUDA programming guide describes. With 14 there are 14m blocks and 28m warps; which m first reaches 112 warps?

CHECK

28m >= 112 gives m = 4, where every SM holds 4 blocks, 8 warps, 2 per scheduler.

Holding is a ceiling, and the wait sets the target

Gathered together, the chains need 768 warps on the course GPU and 112 on the laptop, against 2,304 and 448 held; 768 warps reach 99 percent of the course GPU's full rate; 12.5 percent more work there cost 87 percent more time; and on 14 SMs the literal 48 leaves 6 SMs with 4 blocks and 8 with 3.

Answer the prelab's fourth question in one sentence: when does the sweep reach its largest rate, and what does the multiply-add's wait say about it?

CHECK

The rate peaks once each scheduler has enough independent warps to begin a multiply-add at every chance during the 4-cycle wait: on the course GPU that is 4 per scheduler, 768 warps in all, and on the laptop 2 per scheduler, 112 in all. Past that, extra warps add nothing and can add a round of waiting.

The reading "fill every SM to its maximum" fails twice above: the target is a third of the ceiling, and going past it cost 87 percent more time.

A laptop table with no clean peak comes from the 48 spreading blocks unevenly over 14 SMs, which the reported SM count fixes. A laptop that peaks at a different warp count than the course GPU is correct, because it needs 2 warps per scheduler, not 4. A small step up in warps that costs far more time is a second round of blocks.

Without code, a GPU hides waiting by switching between many independent sets of threads, and it needs only enough of them to cover each wait, here a third or a quarter of what it can hold. Past that point more threads add nothing, and a launch slightly too large can add a whole extra round of time, so "the hardware supports N" is a ceiling, not a target; the right number comes from how long each step of the work waits, and it differs between machines.

The guess at the top has its answer: 112, 56 schedulers with 2 warps each, because a multiply-add waits 4 cycles and 32 threads take 2 cycles to begin one on this GPU.

A harder question to finish with. How would you test, on the course GPU, that the jump from 768 to 864 warps is a second round of blocks and not something else?

CHECK

Compile with -Xptxas -v to see the registers per thread of this kernel, and call cudaOccupancyMaxActiveBlocksPerMultiprocessor for the real kernel at 64 threads per block to get the blocks one SM holds at once. If it says 8, sweep the block count in steps of 1 from a little below 384 = 48 x 8 to a little above it: a second round predicts a step right after 384, not a gradual slope.

The lab pages the 48 in warp_scheduler.cu, 12.5 percent more work, 87 percent more time and fixed by the chip, or made by the launch work these numbers out at length, and the notes on filling the 4-cycle wait and a second round of blocks hold the short versions.
